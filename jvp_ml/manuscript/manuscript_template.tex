\begin{abstract}
\noindent The jugular venous pulse (JVP) reflects right-heart pressures, but at the bedside it is judged by eye and elsewhere measured only invasively. We built a skin-mounted piezoresistive sensor from reduced graphene oxide and copper oxide (rGO/CuO) that records the JVP over the external jugular vein, and asked whether machine learning on the sensor signal alone can identify heart disease. A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) served only as a comparator. The cohort comprised {{N}} participants ({{NPOS}} with physician-verified heart disease, {{NPOS2}} of them from a later recording batch; {{NCYC}} sensor windows). Because the position of each window within the cardiac cycle was not documented, every sensor analysis was made independent of the window's starting point. Under subject-level, repeated, nested cross-validation, the pre-specified primary model, a shift-invariant one-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN), reached an AUROC of {{A:jvp_cnn}} ({{CI:jvp_cnn}}; permutation $P={{CNNPERM_P}}$), and sparse logistic regression reached {{ACI:jvp_l1}}. Models built on 12-lead ECG features reached {{A:ecg_gbm}}--{{A:ecg_rf}}, and a CNN on the 12-lead beat reached {{ACI:ecg_best}}. The sensor CNN {{DLSIG:cnn_ecgbest}} the ECG CNN (DeLong {{DLP:cnn_ecgbest}}). The diseased participants' 12-lead beats, which were parametric reconstructions, resembled one another far more than those of controls (median between-participant correlation {{SIM:dis}} vs {{SIM:ctl}}), so the ECG estimates may be optimistic. A CNN frozen after the first {{TV:ntrain}} participants flagged {{TV:youden|k}} of {{TV:n}} later patients at its pre-specified threshold. Both modalities separated the later-batch patients more easily than the first-batch patients, and that batch contained no controls; on the first batch alone the sensor CNN reached {{SENS:first recording batch only|JVP 1D-CNN}} and 12-lead ECG logistic regression {{SENS:first recording batch only|ECG LR 12-lead}}. The hypothesised ratio of \textit{v}-wave to \textit{c}-wave amplitude discriminated only modestly (AUROC {{VCAUC}}). Models that were not start-agnostic reached an apparent AUROC of {{AL:stored|raw}}, which fell to {{AL:shift|raw}} after random circular shifts of the windows. The sensor signal carries diagnostic information, but these pilot data, with {{NPOS}} diseased participants and an unresolved batch effect, do not show that it matches the 12-lead ECG. A prospective study with concurrently recorded patients and controls and about {{SS:auc0.85|n_pos}} diseased participants is needed.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is still the largest single cause of death in the world. The number of people living with CVD almost doubled between 1990 and 2019, to 523 million \cite{roth2020global}; 19.8 million deaths were attributed to it in 2022 \cite{mensah2023global}; and heart failure (HF) alone affects more than 64 million people \cite{savarese2023global}. Low- and middle-income countries carry a disproportionate share. In India, CVD accounted for 28.1\% of deaths in 2016, and more than half of these deaths occurred before the age of 70 \cite{indiastatelevel2018changing,prabhakaran2016cardiovascular}. Indian patients admitted with HF are younger, and more of them die in hospital, than patients in Western registries \cite{harikrishnan2015clinical}. Pressure or volume overload of the right heart is a frequent part of this picture. Pulmonary hypertension affects roughly 1\% of the global population, and about four in five of those affected live in developing countries \cite{hoeper2016global,humbert2022esc}; rheumatic heart disease, an important cause of right-sided valve disease, remains concentrated in South Asia \cite{watkins2017global}. Conduction disease matters as well. First-degree atrioventricular (AV) block is associated with an approximately threefold higher long-term risk of pacemaker implantation \cite{cheng2009longterm,kerola2019risk}, and high-grade block requires pacing \cite{kusumoto2019acc,glikson2021esc}. All of these conditions are currently detected with an ECG or an echocardiogram read by a specialist, while India has only five to six practising qualified doctors per 10,000 inhabitants, unevenly spread across the country \cite{karan2021size}. Cheap, objective, wearable tools for first-line screening would therefore be useful.

The jugular venous pulse (JVP) is a candidate signal for such a tool. No valve lies between the internal jugular vein and the right atrium, so pulsations in the neck veins follow right-atrial pressure and, through it, right-heart haemodynamics \cite{applefeld1990jugular,chuachiaco2013jugular,constant2000using,devine2007jugular}. A normal JVP tracing has an \textit{a} wave produced by atrial contraction shortly after the P wave of the ECG; a \textit{c} wave and \textit{x} descent associated with tricuspid closure, ventricular systole and atrial relaxation; a \textit{v} wave produced by passive atrial filling while the tricuspid valve is closed, peaking close to the end of the T wave; and a \textit{y} descent when the tricuspid valve opens \cite{applefeld1990jugular,chuachiaco2013jugular,ranganathan2023jugular}. Because each of these components is tied in time to an electrical event, the JVP reflects both mechanics and conduction. A prominent \textit{a} wave points to impaired right-ventricular (RV) filling, as in RV hypertrophy (RVH) and pulmonary hypertension; cannon \textit{a} waves indicate AV dissociation, for example in complete heart block; and large \textit{v} or \textit{cv} waves suggest tricuspid regurgitation and RV volume overload \cite{applefeld1990jugular,devine2007jugular,mcgee2018inspection,morin2017extreme,tung2016cannon,mansoor2015kussmaul}. The JVP also has prognostic value. Among 2,479 patients in the SOLVD trials, an elevated JVP independently predicted hospitalisation for HF and death from pump failure \cite{drazner2001prognostic}, and right-atrial pressure estimated at the neck tracks left-sided filling pressures \cite{drazner2008value,butman1993bedside}. For these reasons, guidelines still rely on it to assess congestion \cite{mcdonagh2021esc,gheorghiade2010assessing,thibodeau2018role}.

At the bedside, however, the JVP is hard to read reliably. The examination depends on the observer and on posture, agreement between observers is poor, and clinicians tend to underestimate venous pressure measured by catheter \cite{cook1990clinical,cook1996does,mcgee1998physical}. The distance from the sternal angle to the right atrium, the usual reference point, varies considerably between patients \cite{seth2002far}. In one series, physical signs taken together detected elevated filling pressures with a sensitivity of only 58\% \cite{stevenson1989limited}; in another, residents recognised a raised right-atrial pressure with 14\% sensitivity, against 82\% for hand-held ultrasound \cite{brennan2007comparison}. In up to 30\% of patients the jugular vein cannot be assessed at all \cite{vinayak2006usefulness,sankoff2008noninvasive}. Ultrasound of the internal jugular vein improves the estimate \cite{simon2010detection,pellicori2014revisiting,pellicori2015prognostic,ammirati2024estimation,rudski2010guidelines}, but it needs a machine and a trained operator and gives a single measurement rather than the continuous \textit{a--c--x--v--y} waveform. The ECG has its own blind spots for right-heart disease. Voltage criteria for RVH \cite{sokolow1949ventricular,hancock2009aha} are specific but insensitive, detecting only 18--44\% of cases in autopsy series \cite{murphy1984reevaluation,lehtonen1988electrocardiographic}, and in MESA-RV their positive predictive value against cardiac MRI did not exceed 12\% \cite{whitman2014validity}. Implanted pressure sensors have shown that continuous haemodynamic data can change outcomes: HF hospitalisations fell in CHAMPION \cite{abraham2011wireless} and in the pre-COVID-19 analysis of GUIDE-HF \cite{lindenfeld2021haemodynamic,zile2008transition,stevenson2010chronic}. These devices are, however, invasive and expensive.

\paragraph{Related work: non-invasive and wearable JVP sensing.}
Table~\ref{tab:prior} summarises the main sensing routes. Amelard and colleagues introduced non-contact optical recording: photoplethysmographic imaging of the neck yielded a venous waveform that ran in antiphase with the carotid pulse \cite{amelard2017noncontact}, optical attenuation over the jugular vein followed invasively measured central venous pressure \cite{amelard2021optical}, and the same approach was used to estimate jugular compliance \cite{amelard2022assessing}. Later work used skin-displacement imaging \cite{lampotang2018noncontact}, camera-based vibrocardiography \cite{moco2017camera,moco2018importance}, Eulerian video magnification compared with right-heart catheterisation \cite{abnousi2019novel}, remote video examination \cite{kelly2020feasibility} and smartphone video \cite{farahani2025noncontact}; ref.~\cite{arrow2023capturing} reviews these methods. All of them need the subject to keep still, stable lighting and a camera facing the neck \cite{saiko2024observation}. In contact optical sensing, reflectance photoplethysmography on the anterior neck was compared with the ECG and with ultrasound \cite{garcialopez2020extracting,garcialopez2020characterization}, and a recent study used machine learning to separate venous from arterial beats in a wearable neck photoplethysmogram \cite{cao2026unmixing}. Ultrasound has been applied in several forms: a conformal ultrasonic patch that captured jugular waveforms \cite{wang2018monitoring}, wearable cardiac imaging and Doppler \cite{hu2023wearable,sempionatto2021epidermal,wang2021flexible,kenny2021novel}, and single-element A-mode tracking of the jugular vein in 65 volunteers \cite{george2022high,george2025single,sahani2016carotid}. Near-field coherent radio-frequency sensing \cite{hui2018monitoring} led to a collar that recorded the JVP with wave timings consistent with the ECG and phonocardiogram \cite{conroy2023jugular}, and a 60-GHz radar has also been tested \cite{das2025feasibility}. The devices most similar to ours are mechanical sensors in contact with the skin: strain-gauge plethysmography of the neck \cite{proto2021plethysmography,menegatti2022effect}, a gyroscope patch \cite{karhinoja2023method}, and a piezoelectric contact sensor whose readings correlated with ultrasound ($r=0.90$) in 20 volunteers \cite{george2024jugular}. Flexible electronic-skin devices, such as a capacitive sensor with microhair structures \cite{pang2015highly}, a carbon-nanotube/PDMS piezoresistive film \cite{tai2015flexible} and a piezoelectret array on the neck \cite{han2022mapping}, have shown the jugular pulse only as a demonstration signal.

\paragraph{Related work: flexible piezoresistive materials.}
Flexible pressure sensors developed from microstructured dielectrics and transistor designs \cite{schwartz2013flexible,wang2014silk}, interlocking nanostructures \cite{pang2012flexible}, nanowire and piezoelectric films \cite{gong2014wearable,dagdeviren2014conformable,park2017self} and bioresorbable sensors for arterial flow \cite{boutry2019biodegradable}; reviews are given in refs.~\cite{chortos2016pursuing,ray2019bio,meng2022wearable}. Piezoresistors made of graphene or reduced graphene oxide (rGO) offer large gauge factors and can be processed from solution at low cost. Examples include graphene--polyurethane sponges \cite{yao2013flexible}, laser-scribed graphene and graphene with a spinosum-like microstructure \cite{tian2015graphene,pang2018epidermis}, rGO paper and rGO-coated fabrics \cite{tao2017graphene,li2019wearable}, MXene and MXene/rGO aerogels \cite{ma2017highly,ma2018synergistical} and copper nanowire networks wrapped in rGO \cite{zhu2022epidermis}. Nearly all of these devices were demonstrated on the radial or carotid arterial pulse, whose amplitude is about ten times that of the venous pulse. We are not aware of a previous report of an rGO/CuO hybrid used as a piezoresistive sensor for the venous pulse.

\paragraph{Related work: machine learning on cardiac waveforms.}
Deep learning has changed how ECGs are interpreted. A one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) trained on 91,232 recordings classified rhythms as well as cardiologists \cite{hannun2019cardiologist}; a network trained on two million tracings read the 12-lead ECG \cite{ribeiro2020automatic}; and ECG models now detect conditions that human readers cannot see, including LV systolic dysfunction \cite{attia2019screening,yao2021artificial}, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation recorded in sinus rhythm \cite{attia2019artificial}, LV hypertrophy \cite{kwon2020comparing}, pulmonary hypertension \cite{kwon2020artificial,aras2023electrocardiogram}, RV dysfunction \cite{vaid2022using} and future complete heart block \cite{sau2025artificial}. Reviews and benchmarks are available in refs.~\cite{siontis2021artificial,somani2021deep,topol2019high,wagner2020ptbxl,strodthoff2021deep}. For mechanical and haemodynamic waveforms the field is younger. Photoplethysmography from smartwatches detects atrial fibrillation \cite{tison2018passive,perez2019large} and has been used to screen for diabetes \cite{avram2020digital}; analysis of the arterial pulse wave is the basis of cuffless blood-pressure estimation \cite{mukkamala2015toward,elhajj2020review,kim2020detection}; and seismocardiography combined with machine learning has been used to grade HF and to estimate intracardiac pressures \cite{inan2015ballistocardiography,inan2018novel,shandhi2022estimation,klein2025noninvasive,etemadi2016wearable,stehlik2020continuous,lee2020mechano,ha2019chest,kaisti2019clinical}. These studies used thousands to millions of labelled recordings. In JVP research, machine learning has so far been applied to signal processing, namely source separation in neck photoplethysmography \cite{cao2026unmixing} and segmentation of jugular ultrasound \cite{zamboni2026artificial}, and not to diagnosis.

\paragraph{Gap and contributions.}
The JVP has thus been recorded optically, ultrasonically, with radio-frequency sensors and with mechanical sensors, yet nearly every study reports waveform shape or timing agreement in a small group of healthy volunteers. We found no skin-conformal wearable JVP sensor that had been used to classify heart disease with machine learning and compared against the ECG. This study makes four contributions. (i) We recorded JVP windows in {{N}} participants with an rGO/CuO piezoresistive sensor placed over the external jugular vein, alongside a reference 12-lead ECG, and analysed the sensor data with methods that assume nothing about where a window starts. (ii) We turned two clinical hypotheses, that RV disease raises the \textit{v} wave relative to the \textit{c} wave and that delayed conduction flattens the \textit{a}$\to$\textit{c} transition, into automatically measured waveform features and tested them. (iii) Treating the sensor as a standalone device and the ECG purely as a comparator, we compared interpretable models, random-kernel and convolutional networks and a physiology-hybrid network on the sensor with models on the 12-lead ECG, using subject-level repeated nested cross-validation, bootstrap confidence intervals, permutation tests and DeLong comparisons in line with TRIPOD+AI \cite{collins2024tripod}; we also examined how the 1:{{IMBRATIO}} class imbalance affects discrimination, operating points and calibration. (iv) We applied a model frozen after the first recording batch, without changes, to a later batch of patients and examined whether the recording batch shapes the results. With only {{NPOS}} diseased participants, the study is a feasibility analysis, and we report its uncertainty explicitly \cite{varoquaux2018cross,riley2020calculating}.

Labels were assigned per participant as diseased ($n={{NPOS}}$) or not diseased ($n={{NNEG}}$) from a physician's diagnosis, which was independently verified \authnote{specify the diagnostic work-up (e.g. 12-lead ECG, echocardiography), the number and specialty of the physicians, whether they had access to the sensor recordings, the specific diagnoses (RVH, heart block, other) of the {{NPOS}} diseased participants, inclusion/exclusion criteria, age, sex and BMI, and ethics approval}. Each participant contributed two to four short sensor windows (current and calibrated force; 0.6--1.1\,s, roughly one cardiac period each) and one reference beat for each of the 12 ECG leads (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). After exact duplicates were removed, {{NCYC}} distinct windows remained (median {{SPC}} samples per window, range {{SPC_RANGE}}). The position of each window within the cardiac cycle is not documented. We therefore made no assumption about where a window begins and did not use window duration: every sensor analysis was built to be unaffected by the starting point (Methods). Force was a linear transform of current within each recording (median $|r|={{RIF}}$), so we analysed force and checked that raw current gave the same results.

An audit of the workbooks (Supplementary Table~S1) found the kind of problems that arise when datasets are assembled by hand, and we dealt with each of them explicitly. The 12-lead ECGs of {{NQCEXCL_WORD}} non-diseased participants ({{QCEXCL}}) were identical to those of other participants. For HS1 and HS35 this is new in the 12-lead files, whose V1 and V2 traces also differ from the same participants' earlier single-lead workbooks; for the other four the copies were already present in the earlier V1/V2 data. V1 and V2 of the 12-lead files differed from the single-lead workbooks for four further participants as well. In these cases we used the 12-lead file, which holds all leads on one time axis. Time stamps of three 12-lead files were stored in milliseconds and were converted to seconds. Four participants had a sensor window stored twice under different cycle numbers, and one window differed between two workbooks of the same participant. All analyses used the curated data. In a sensitivity analysis we excluded the {{NQCEXCL_WORD}} participants with copied ECGs.

\subsection*{Sensor waveform morphology}
Since we do not know where each window starts within the cardiac cycle, we rotated every window so that its deepest trough, taken as the \textit{x} descent, sat at the origin. The prominent peaks then follow in the physiological order \textit{x}$\to$\textit{v}$\to$\textit{y}$\to$\textit{a}$\to$\textit{c} (Fig.~\ref{fig:waves}a,b). The class-mean waveforms overlapped considerably (Fig.~\ref{fig:waves}c). In non-diseased participants the signal fell smoothly from the \textit{v} wave towards the next \textit{x} trough; in diseased participants it tended to drop earlier and more steeply after the \textit{v} wave (\textit{y} descent) and then showed a late shoulder before returning to \textit{x}. In lead V1, the class-mean ECG of the diseased participants had a deeper S wave (Fig.~\ref{fig:waves}d).

\subsection*{Testing the physiological hypotheses}
Diseased participants had a higher \textit{v}/\textit{c} amplitude ratio (median {{VC_MED_DIS}} vs {{VC_MED_CTL}}; Mann--Whitney $P={{VCP}}$, unadjusted), but the ratio separated the groups only modestly (AUROC {{VCAUC}}, 95\% bootstrap CI {{VCCI}}; Fig.~\ref{fig:features}a). Applied as a rule, \textit{v}/\textit{c}$>$1 identified {{RULE_TP}} of {{NPOS}} diseased participants (sensitivity {{RULE_SENS}}) with a specificity of {{RULE_SPEC}} ({{RULE_TN}} of {{NNEG}}). The \textit{a}$\to$\textit{c} slope did not differ significantly between groups (AUROC {{AUC_SLOPE}}, $P={{P_SLOPE}}$). When we screened all 33 shift-invariant sensor features, the clearest separation came from the sine of the third-harmonic relative phase, which describes how the large and small waves are arranged within the period (AUROC {{AUC_REL3}}, $P={{P_REL3}}$; significant after Bonferroni correction for 33 features). The sine of the second-harmonic relative phase came next (AUROC {{AUC_REL2}}, $P={{P_REL2}}$; also significant after correction), and the \textit{y} descent was deeper in diseased participants (AUROC {{AUC_YD}}, $P={{P_YD}}$, unadjusted).

On the 12-lead ECG, diseased participants had a deeper S wave in V1 (AUROC {{AUC_V1S}}), as the hypothesis of a sharp S wave predicted, a taller R wave in V4 (median {{EMED:V4_R_mV|dis}} vs {{EMED:V4_R_mV|ctl}}\,mV; AUROC {{EAUC:V4_R_mV}}), a higher Sokolow--Lyon LVH voltage ({{EMED:SokolowLyon_LVH_mV|dis}} vs {{EMED:SokolowLyon_LVH_mV|ctl}}\,mV; AUROC {{EAUC:SokolowLyon_LVH_mV}}) and a longer beat ({{EMED:beat_ms|dis}} vs {{EMED:beat_ms|ctl}}\,ms; AUROC {{EAUC:beat_ms}}). Of the continuous 12-lead features, {{ECG_NUNI_SIG}} remained significant after Bonferroni correction. The standard criteria for RVH, by contrast, were of little use: any of the six voltage and axis criteria was met by {{RVH_TP}} of {{NPOS}} diseased participants (sensitivity {{RVH_SENS}}, specificity {{RVH_SPEC}}), and none of the diseased participants had right-axis deviation or R in V1 $\geq$0.7\,mV. This is consistent with the known insensitivity of these criteria \cite{murphy1984reevaluation,whitman2014validity}. The Sokolow--Lyon LVH criterion was met by {{CRIT:LVH_crit_SL_ge_3.5|dis}} diseased and {{CRIT:LVH_crit_SL_ge_3.5|ctl}} non-diseased participants.

\subsection*{Sensor models compared with 12-lead ECG models}
Table~\ref{tab:bench} and Fig.~\ref{fig:bench} show every model under subject-level repeated stratified three-fold cross-validation (each test fold holds {{NPOS_FOLD}} diseased participants), with hyper-parameters and thresholds chosen inside each training fold. The 1D-CNN was chosen as the primary sensor model during the analysis of the first batch, where it was the best of eleven sensor models, and it was kept unchanged. In the full cohort its AUROC was {{A:jvp_cnn}} ({{CI:jvp_cnn}}; AUPRC {{PR:jvp_cnn}}, against {{PREV}} for chance). Several other start-agnostic models performed similarly: the physiology-hybrid CNN ({{ACIP:jvp_hcnn}}), sparse logistic regression ({{ACIP:jvp_l1}}), circular ROCKET ({{ACIP:jvp_rocket}}), logistic regression on the Fourier descriptors ({{ACIP:jvp_fourier}}) and a random forest ({{ACIP:jvp_rf}}). Logistic regression on all 33 features without selection did less well ({{ACIP:jvp_lr}}; permutation $P={{PERM:jvp_lr}}$), as expected when the features outnumber the diseased participants almost three to one, and the \textit{v}/\textit{c} rule reached {{ACI:rule}}. The primary model was picked after a comparison on the first batch, which makes up most of the present cohort, so its estimate is still optimistic.

The ECG models had the full 12-lead beat. Logistic regression on the {{ECG_NFEAT}} features of the 12-lead ECG, fixed in advance as the reference comparator, reached {{ACI:ecg_lr}}; the other feature-based models ranged from {{A:ecg_gbm}} (gradient boosting) to {{A:ecg_rf}} (random forest). \subsection*{Class imbalance}
With {{NPOS}} diseased among {{N}} participants (prevalence {{PREVPCT}}\%), a classifier that calls everyone non-diseased is {{ACCALLNEG}}\% accurate. We therefore report only metrics that are robust to imbalance, namely AUROC, AUPRC (chance level {{PREV}}), MCC and balanced accuracy \cite{saito2015precision,chicco2020advantages}, and tested whether correcting the imbalance changes the conclusions (Supplementary Table~S2 and Fig.~S3). Every correction was applied inside the training folds only, including the inner tuning folds, so that synthetic or re-weighted samples never reached test participants. The stored windows are not placed at random within the cardiac cycle. \subsection*{How like-for-like is the ECG comparison?}
Deep-learning models on recorded 12-lead ECGs detect pulmonary hypertension and RV dysfunction with AUROCs of 0.84--0.90 \cite{kwon2020artificial,aras2023electrocardiogram,vaid2022using}. The 12-lead ECG models in this study reached similar or higher values ({{A:ecg_gbm}}--{{A:ecg_best}}), although their input differs from a clinical recording in four respects (Supplementary Fig.~S2). First, the ECG files are not recorded signals. A six-Gaussian P--Q--R--S--S$'$--T model reproduced them with a median $R^2$ of {{EI:r2}} ({{EI:r2n}} of {{EI:nleads}} leads above 0.999), and a median {{EI:flat}}\% of each beat was exactly flat. Second, the limb leads are not mutually consistent. In a simultaneous recording lead III equals II $-$ I (Einthoven's law), and the augmented leads follow from I and II \cite{goldberger1942simple}. Here the median deviation from Einthoven's law was {{EI:einth}}\% of the lead-II amplitude, and only {{EI:einth10}} of the {{EI:limbn}} participants whose ECG was not a copy were within 10\%. Third, the beats carry no conduction information: the PR interval in lead II ranged from {{EI:prlo}} to {{EI:prhi}}\,ms, below the physiological minimum of 120\,ms in all {{EI:pr120}} participants, and none exceeded the 200\,ms threshold for first-degree AV block, so no model could have detected AV conduction delay from these beats. Fourth, the beats of diseased participants were strikingly alike. The median correlation between the 12-lead beats of two diseased participants was {{SIM:dis}} ({{SIM:dis1}} within the first batch and {{SIM:dis2}} within the later batch), against {{SIM:ctl}} between two non-diseased participants and {{SIM:x}} between a diseased and a non-diseased participant; {{SIM:dis90}} of {{SIM:disn}} diseased pairs, but only {{SIM:ctl90}} of {{SIM:ctln}} non-diseased pairs, correlated above 0.9. A shared disease phenotype may explain part of this similarity. In reconstructed beats, however, it could also arise from a common template, and a CNN on the whole beat is well placed to exploit it \authnote{explain how the 12-lead beats were produced and whether the same template or parameter set was used for several participants}. A crude start-agnostic \textit{v}/\textit{c} ratio from the raw sensor samples agreed with the diagnosis in {{EI:agree}} of {{N}} participants (AUROC {{EI:auc}}). Both modalities therefore carry a signal in these data, but the comparison cannot establish how the sensor would perform against a recorded clinical 12-lead ECG.

\section*{Discussion}
A skin-mounted rGO/CuO piezoresistive sensor recorded jugular venous pulse waveforms from which start-agnostic models separated participants with physician-verified heart disease from those without. The pre-specified primary model, a 1D-CNN, reached an AUROC of {{A:jvp_cnn}}, sparse logistic regression reached {{A:jvp_l1}} (permutation $P={{PERM:jvp_l1}}$), and a model frozen after the first batch flagged {{TV:youden|k}} of {{TV:n}} later patients. Now that a 12-lead ECG was available for every participant, the ECG comparator was stronger than in our two-lead analysis, mainly through a CNN that reads the whole 12-lead beat. Feature-based 12-lead models performed in the same range as the sensor ({{A:ecg_gbm}}--{{A:ecg_rf}}), whereas a CNN on the 12-lead beat reached {{A:ecg_best}}, and the sensor CNN {{DLSIG:cnn_ecgbest}} it. On the first batch alone, where the batch confound is absent, the sensor CNN ({{SENS:first recording batch only|JVP 1D-CNN}}) and 12-lead ECG logistic regression ({{SENS:first recording batch only|ECG LR 12-lead}}) were nearly identical. In our literature search we found no earlier wearable JVP sensor that had been combined with machine-learning classification of heart disease and compared with the ECG. Previous wearable and non-contact JVP studies reported agreement of waveform or timing in healthy volunteers \cite{amelard2017noncontact,garcialopez2020extracting,conroy2023jugular,karhinoja2023method,george2024jugular,george2025single} or used machine learning to separate signals \cite{cao2026unmixing}.

Methodologically, the main lesson concerns alignment. The apparent accuracy of a JVP analysis can depend strongly on where the recorded windows sit within the cardiac cycle. A model that read the raw window discriminated almost perfectly on the stored windows, and the advantage vanished once the windows were rotated at random, while truly shift-invariant representations were unaffected. Any analysis that looks for waves at fixed positions within a window is exposed to the same problem. Positional information is not necessarily spurious, since the timing of the venous waves relative to atrial and ventricular events carries physiological meaning \cite{applefeld1990jugular,chuachiaco2013jugular}. It can only be used, however, if windows are cut from the sensor signal by a documented rule or are synchronised with a simultaneously recorded ECG, and it then has to be validated as such. We recommend that wearable JVP studies describe how they segment the signal and include an alignment test like the one reported here.

The physiology turned out to be less simple than the original hypotheses suggested. After alignment information was removed, the \textit{v}/\textit{c} ratio on its own discriminated only modestly and the \textit{a}$\to$\textit{c} slope did not separate the groups significantly. Diseased participants did tend to have higher \textit{v}/\textit{c} ratios and deeper \textit{y} descents, as expected with RV volume overload and tricuspid regurgitation \cite{applefeld1990jugular,devine2007jugular,ranganathan2023jugular}. The strongest single descriptors measured waveform asymmetry (the relative phases of the second and third harmonics). This suggests that the arrangement of the venous waves within the beat, more than any single amplitude ratio, carries the diagnostic information; because the finding came from an exploratory screen, it needs confirmation. The best sensor models were those that restrict their effective complexity: the 1D-CNN with shared convolutional weights, random-kernel ROCKET and sparse logistic regression. Unpenalised use of all 33 features did worse, in keeping with the evidence that model complexity has to match the number of events in small clinical datasets \cite{christodoulou2019systematic,grinsztajn2022why,riley2020calculating}.

The 12-lead ECG comparison needs careful reading. The ECG files were parametric reconstructions with internally inconsistent limb leads and no conduction information, and the beats of diseased participants were far more alike than those of controls. The last point matters most. A CNN given the whole 12-lead beat can learn a shared template directly, which may explain why it outperformed every feature-based ECG model and why all ECG models, unlike in our two-lead analysis, found the later-batch patients easier to classify. A genuine common phenotype among the diseased participants could produce the same pattern, and these data cannot distinguish the two explanations. The classical voltage and axis criteria for RVH flagged almost none of the diseased participants, which fits their known insensitivity \cite{murphy1984reevaluation,lehtonen1988electrocardiographic,whitman2014validity}. The weak coupling between sensor and ECG features indicates that the two modalities capture partly different information. The sensor is meant to be used alone, so the ECG served only to show what conventional electrical screening achieves in the same participants. A fair test will require recorded, synchronised 12-lead ECGs.

The class-imbalance analysis has a practical consequence for deployment. Re-weighting or resampling was not needed to rank participants correctly, but the decision threshold has to be set deliberately for the intended prevalence, and models trained with imbalance corrections must be recalibrated before their outputs are interpreted as risks \cite{vandengoorbergh2022harm}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} This study has important limitations, and its results should be treated as proof of concept. First, the cohort is small and imbalanced. With {{NPOS}} diseased participants the confidence intervals are wide and the estimates may not generalise \cite{varoquaux2018cross,vabalas2019machine,riley2020calculating}. Second, half of the diseased participants came from a later batch that had no controls and was sampled more sparsely, so disease and recording batch are partly confounded. Both modalities separated the later patients more easily, and we cannot say whether this reflects their disease or the way the data were acquired. Third, the analysis was revised after results had been seen. The start-agnostic design, the trough-anchored fiducials and two of the models (sparse logistic regression and the Fourier-descriptor model) were introduced in a re-analysis of the first batch, and the 1D-CNN became the primary sensor model because it performed best of eleven in that batch, so its AUROC is optimistic. The temporal check of the frozen model addresses sensitivity only. Fourth, the diseased group combines different conditions (RVH and heart block) \authnote{confirm diagnoses}, so condition-specific performance cannot be reported, and a pilot cohort of clear-cut patients and volunteers may be easier to separate than unselected patients (spectrum bias) \cite{wolff2019probast,bossuyt2015stard}. Fifth, the sensor windows were short (one beat), sampled at only about 5--27\,Hz and of undocumented position within the cardiac cycle, and the 12-lead ECG beats were reconstructions rather than synchronous recordings, which ruled out timing analyses; some automatically measured ECG intervals were physiologically implausible. Sixth, several workbooks contained copy errors, including six copied ECGs; excluding the affected participants did not change the conclusions. Finally, all data come from one site and have not been validated externally. The next step is a prospective, multi-centre study that recruits and records patients and controls concurrently with an identical protocol, with continuous synchronised sensor and 12-lead ECG acquisition at $\geq$100\,Hz, sensor-only segmentation specified in advance, echocardiographic reference standards, condition-specific labels and blinded assessment, reported according to TRIPOD+AI and STARD-AI \cite{collins2024tripod,sounderajah2025stard,bossuyt2015stard,wolff2019probast,kelly2019key,liu2020reporting}.

An rGO/CuO piezoresistive neck sensor thus turns the jugular venous pulse, so far a subjective bedside sign, into a quantitative waveform. Analysed without any assumption about window alignment, this waveform separated participants with heart disease from those without, at a level similar to that of feature-based 12-lead ECG models and numerically, though not significantly, below that of a 12-lead ECG CNN in this pilot cohort. The estimates are imprecise and partly confounded by recording batch, and the ECG comparator was built from reconstructed beats. Low-cost wearable JVP sensing with start-agnostic machine learning deserves prospective validation as a screening aid in settings where specialists and echocardiography are scarce.

\subsection*{Data curation}
The current data delivery numbers the participants HS1--HS58. Earlier deliveries had used different numbers for seven participants (Supplementary Note~1), and the sensor windows were identical between deliveries. We therefore matched each participant to the earlier deliveries by a content hash of the sensor windows; all 170 windows matched, and each participant kept the recording batch in which their windows had first been received. Workbooks were parsed by locating columns from their header text, because column positions varied between files. Sensor windows that appeared in more than one workbook of a participant were counted once (content hash). The ECG was read from the 12-lead workbook, which stores all leads on one time axis; time stamps stored in milliseconds were converted to seconds. Leads V1 and V2 of the 12-lead workbook were compared with the corresponding single-lead workbooks of the earlier delivery. ECG beats or sensor windows identical between participants, and workbook headers naming another participant, were flagged (Supplementary Table~S1). Both recording batches were parsed with the same code, and each participant's batch was kept for the batch analyses. No participant was excluded from the primary analysis.

\subsection*{Signal processing and features}
Sensor features were computed from the sensor signal alone; ECG timing was never used to locate JVP waves. Each sensor window was interpolated to 64 points with monotone piecewise cubic (PCHIP) interpolation \cite{fritsch1980monotone}, which does not overshoot between sparse samples, and min--max normalised to remove differences in contact pressure and sensor gain between people. Since the starting point of a window within the cardiac cycle is unknown, we treated every window as one period of a periodic signal and made each feature invariant to circular shifts. \textit{Trough-anchored fiducials.} The window was rotated so that its deepest trough, taken to be the \textit{x} descent, lay at the origin. Prominent peaks (prominence at least 0.08 of the range, found circularly) then appear in the physiological order \textit{x}$\to$\textit{v}$\to$(\textit{y})$\to$\textit{a}$\to$\textit{c}: \textit{v} is the first prominent peak after \textit{x}, \textit{c} the last one before the return to \textit{x}, \textit{a} the peak before \textit{c} when there are three or more peaks, and \textit{y} the minimum between \textit{v} and the following peak. When the window had only one dominant hump, \textit{v} and \textit{c} were taken as the maxima of the first and second halves after \textit{x}. From these points we derived wave heights above \textit{x}; the ratios \textit{v}/\textit{c}, \textit{v}/\textit{a} and \textit{c}/\textit{a}, each regularised by adding 0.05 to numerator and denominator; the depth of the \textit{y} descent; the phase distances \textit{x}$\to$\textit{v}, \textit{v}$\to$\textit{c} and \textit{c}$\to$\textit{x}; the \textit{a}$\to$\textit{c} slope; the slopes of the \textit{v} upstroke and the \textit{x} descent; and the number of prominent peaks. \textit{Fourier descriptors.} We used the normalised magnitudes of harmonics 1--6 and the relative phases $\phi_k-k\phi_1$ ($k=2,3,4$; as cosine and sine). These do not change under circular shifts and describe the shape and asymmetry of the waveform. \textit{Distribution and texture.} Skewness, kurtosis, the fraction of the window above half its range and circular roughness. \textit{Beat-to-beat measures.} The standard deviation of \textit{v}/\textit{c} across windows and the mean maximum circular cross-correlation between windows. The 33 features were summarised per participant by the median over windows.

For the ECG, each lead was baseline-corrected and the following were measured: R and S amplitudes (within 60\,ms of the steepest QRS slope), R/S ratio, R$-|$S$|$, QRS duration (onset at the PR-segment minimum before the first QRS deflection, offset at the return to within 15\% of the QRS amplitude), P and T amplitudes, PR interval (P onset at 10\% of the P amplitude) and QT interval. Global 12-lead measures were the median QRS duration over the 12 leads, PR and QT intervals in lead II, the QT interval corrected with Bazett's formula using the beat length as the RR interval \cite{bazett1920analysis}, the frontal QRS axis from the net QRS deflections in leads I and aVF, the Sokolow--Lyon voltages for RVH (R in V1 plus S in V5 or V6) \cite{sokolow1949ventricular} and LVH (S in V1 plus R in V5 or V6) \cite{sokolow1949lvh}, and the R/S ratio in V6. Binary voltage and axis criteria were added: for RVH, R in V1 $\geq$0.7\,mV, R/S in V1 $\geq$1, R in V1 plus S in V5/V6 $>$1.05\,mV, S in V5 or V6 $\geq$0.7\,mV, R/S in V6 $\leq$1 and a frontal axis beyond $+90^{\circ}$; for LVH, a Sokolow--Lyon voltage $\geq$3.5\,mV \cite{hancock2009aha,kligfield2007recommendations}. This gave {{ECG_NFEAT}} ECG features. The two-lead comparator of our earlier analysis used the {{ECG_NFEAT2}} features of V1 and V2 only. To check the internal consistency of the limb leads we computed, for each participant, the largest absolute deviation from Einthoven's law (III $=$ II $-$ I) and from Goldberger's relations for the augmented leads \cite{goldberger1942simple}, relative to the peak-to-peak amplitude of lead II. None of the feature extraction used labels, so no label information could pass between folds.

\subsection*{Models}
\textit{Rule.} The hypothesised rule (diseased if \textit{v}/\textit{c}$>$1) was applied without any training. \textit{Tabular models.} Logistic regression (L2 penalty, class-balanced), a support vector machine (RBF kernel, scored by its margin), a random forest (500 trees) \cite{breiman2001random} and gradient boosting \cite{friedman2001greedy}, each preceded by median imputation and standardisation, were tuned by an inner stratified three-fold grid search on AUROC \cite{cawley2010overfitting,vabalas2019machine} with scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. The decision threshold was set by the Youden index on the inner out-of-fold scores. Sparse (L1) logistic regression, which selects features within each training fold, and L2 logistic regression on the 12 Fourier descriptors alone were added during the start-agnostic re-analysis of the first batch, because the full feature set contains far more features than there are diseased participants. \textit{ROCKET.} One thousand random convolutional kernels \cite{dempster2020rocket} were applied circularly to each trough-anchored window, so that the proportion-of-positive-values and maximum features are exactly invariant to circular shifts, followed by strongly regularised logistic regression; window scores were averaged per participant. \textit{1D-CNN.} A network of three convolutional blocks with circular padding (16, 32 and 32 channels; kernel sizes 7, 5 and 3; batch normalisation; global average pooling; dropout 0.3; about 9,000 parameters) received the normalised window and its circular derivative as two channels \cite{kiranyaz2021onedimensional,ismailfawaz2019deep}. During training the windows were shifted circularly at random, and at test time predictions were averaged over eight evenly spaced circular shifts, which makes the network start-agnostic. Training ran for 120 epochs with AdamW \cite{loshchilov2019decoupled} (learning rate $3\times10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$, cosine schedule) on a binary cross-entropy loss weighted towards the positive class, with on-the-fly augmentation by smooth time warping, amplitude scaling, jitter and baseline wander \cite{um2017data,iwana2021empirical}. Three seeds were ensembled and the participant's probability was the mean over windows. \textit{Physiology-hybrid CNN.} The CNN embedding was joined, before the output layer, with the two standardised features that encode the prior clinical hypotheses (\textit{v}/\textit{c} ratio and \textit{a}$\to$\textit{c} slope). \textit{ECG models.} The same tabular learners, including sparse logistic regression, were trained on the {{ECG_NFEAT}} 12-lead features, and a CNN of the same architecture (without circular padding) was trained on the 12-lead beat resampled to 200 samples per lead (12 input channels). Logistic regression and random forest on the V1/V2 features were kept as secondary comparators to show what the additional leads contribute. Before running the analysis we fixed 12-lead logistic regression as the reference ECG comparator, because it was the reference in the earlier two-lead analysis; the ECG model with the highest AUROC is reported as well. No model combined sensor and ECG inputs, because the intended system uses the sensor alone. All models were implemented in Python with scikit-learn and PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch}.

\subsection*{Class imbalance}
The primary models used class-balanced weights (tabular learners) or a positive-class-weighted cross-entropy (CNNs), with thresholds chosen inside the training folds. In a separate analysis we compared, for logistic regression and random forests on sensor and 12-lead ECG features, no correction, class weighting, SMOTE \cite{chawla2002smote} (synthetic positives interpolated between a positive participant and one of its three nearest positive neighbours until the classes were balanced) and an undersampling ensemble of ten models, each trained on all positives and an equal number of randomly drawn negatives \cite{liu2009exploratory}. For the sensor 1D-CNN we compared unweighted and positive-weighted cross-entropy, class-balanced focal loss ($\gamma=2$) \cite{lin2017focal} and oversampling of positive windows to parity. Resampling was built into the estimators, so it was applied inside both outer and inner folds and never touched test data \cite{he2009learning}. We report AUROC, AUPRC, MCC \cite{chicco2020advantages}, sensitivity and specificity at a threshold of 0.5 and at the Youden threshold chosen in the training fold, the Brier score and calibration-in-the-large (mean predicted risk minus prevalence) \cite{vancalster2019calibration}. Sensitivity at fixed specificity and specificity at 100\% sensitivity were computed on the out-of-fold scores of each repeat and then averaged. Predictive values at other prevalences were obtained from the cross-validated sensitivity and specificity with Bayes' theorem, and sample sizes from the Hanley--McNeil standard error of the AUROC \cite{hanley1982meaning,riley2020calculating}.

\subsection*{Evaluation and statistics}
All data splits were made at participant level, so that windows of one person never appeared in both training and test data \cite{saeb2017need,kapoor2023leakage}. We used repeated stratified three-fold cross-validation (30 repeats for fast models; 10 for CNN, ROCKET and tree models), with all tuning and threshold selection nested inside the training folds. In each repeat, out-of-fold predictions for all participants were pooled to compute AUROC, average precision (AUPRC, which is informative under class imbalance \cite{saito2015precision}), sensitivity, specificity, balanced accuracy and Brier score. Point estimates are means over repeats; 95\% CIs come from 2,000 stratified participant-level bootstrap resamples of that mean \cite{efron1979bootstrap,efron1993introduction}. Paired AUROC comparisons used DeLong's test on repeat-averaged scores \cite{delong1988comparing}. Performance against chance was tested with permutation tests that repeated the whole cross-validation on {{NPERM}} label permutations \cite{ojala2010permutation}. For the 1D-CNN, whose full configuration is too slow to repeat hundreds of times, both the observed statistic and the null distribution ({{CNNPERM_N}} permutations) were computed with one cross-validation pass and one seed. Group differences in single features were tested with two-sided Mann--Whitney tests and are reported without adjustment for multiplicity, as exploratory results. To measure how strongly an analysis that is not start-agnostic could depend on where the windows happen to begin, we compared logistic regression on the raw 64-point window with logistic regression on the shift-invariant Fourier descriptors, on the windows as stored and after a random circular shift of every window (20 repeats). Effects of recording batch were examined by (i) applying the 1D-CNN trained on the first batch, with operating thresholds derived from its cross-validated scores, unchanged to the later batch (the model had been frozen before the later data arrived; sensitivity with Clopper--Pearson 95\% CIs); (ii) computing from the pooled out-of-fold scores the AUROC of each batch's diseased participants against all non-diseased participants; (iii) repeating the core analyses on the first batch alone and with the first-batch diseased participants replaced by those of the later batch; and (iv) testing whether start-agnostic features separate the diseased participants of the two batches (repeated stratified three-fold cross-validation, 30 repeats) and whether the sampling rate follows the diagnosis or the CNN score. Robustness of the 1D-CNN, sparse logistic regression and the \textit{v}/\textit{c} rule was assessed by training on clean data and testing on held-out folds with added Gaussian noise (30 to 0\,dB SNR) or after decimation, over 10 repeats. Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI \cite{collins2024tripod}.
